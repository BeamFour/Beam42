Equally Inclined Chords

How Hopkins turns two traced rays into an optical path difference without ever constructing a wavefront — and where the Java follows his paper exactly, where it simplifies, and where it disagrees.

Checked line by line against H. H. Hopkins, Calculation of the aberrations and image assessment for a general optical system. Equation numbers below are the paper's.

01
Paper §3
eq. 3.1, 3.4

What the OPD actually is

Hopkins opens by defining the wave aberration as a difference of two optical path lengths, taken between two reference spheres:

eq. 3.1
W′ = [ E P₁ P₂ … Pk E′ ] − [ B P₁ P₂ … Pk B′ ]
QEP₁…PkE′ is the principal ray (the chief ray); QBP₁…PkB′ is a general aperture ray. B and B′ are where the two rays cut the object- and image-side reference spheres, centred on Q and the chosen image point.

Note the order: principal minus aperture. That single detail fixes the sign convention of every formula downstream, and of WaveAbr's return value.

Two things make (3.1) awkward to evaluate by brute force, and the paper's abstract names both:

  • Indeterminacies. With the object, image or either pupil at infinity, the individual path lengths and pupil coordinates diverge. Hopkins' stated goal is formulae that "never involve indeterminacies".
  • Cancellation. The paths run to hundreds of millimetres; their difference is a fraction of a micron. Forming the large sums first throws away most of the significant digits — a serious matter in 1981, a mild one now.

His answer is a construction that produces the difference region by region, without ever building the large sums.

02
eq. 3.9
eic_distance()

The equally inclined chord

What you start from

Four quantities, all of them straight out of the ray trace:

ray r — the aperture rayray r₀ — the principal ray
a point on it P = (X, Y, Z) P₀ = (X₀, Y₀, Z₀)
its unit direction d = (L, M, N) d₀ = (L₀, M₀, N₀)

In practice P and P₀ are the two rays' intersections with the same surface, and d, d₀ their direction cosines there, in whatever rectangular frame the trace happens to use. Two things you are never given, and never need: where the two rays cross, and any wavefront. That is what makes the construction usable.

The one unknown

P₀ stays put. You slide along ray r only, looking for the point P̃ whose chord back to P₀ is equally inclined to both rays — so there is one unknown and one equation. Parametrise the slide:

P̃ = P − ed      that is   ( X − Le, Y − Me, Z − Ne )
e is the distance from P̃ back to P, positive when that step runs along +d. It is the quantity eq. 3.9 returns.

The condition, and why it has a sign flip

Hopkins writes the equal-inclination requirement as Σ L {(X − Le) − X₀} = Σ L₀ {X₀ − (X − Le)}, which in vectors is

d·( P̃ − P₀ ) = d₀·( P₀ − P̃ )   ⟺   ( d + d₀ ) · ( P̃ − P₀ ) = 0
The chord is reversed on the right, and the two statements are equivalent. The second is the one worth remembering: the chord is perpendicular to the bisector of the two ray directions.

That reversal is not a slip. Picture the intersecting case: the rays cross at X, and P̃, P₀ sit equidistant from it, so triangle X P̃ P₀ is isosceles. Its two base angles are equal — but they sit at opposite ends of the base and open toward each other. Traversing the chord P₀→P̃ makes the same angle with d that traversing P̃→P₀ makes with d₀. Hence one direction each way.

Solving for e

One substitution and one dot product. Put P̃ = P − ed into the condition and expand:

( d + d₀ ) · ( P − ed − P₀ ) = 0
( d + d₀ )·( P − P₀ )  −  e ( d + d₀ )·d  =  0
The coefficient of e collapses, because d is a unit vector: ( d + d₀ )·d = d·d + d₀·d = 1 + d·d₀.
( d + d₀ )·( P − P₀ )  =  e ( 1 + d·d₀ )

Divide, and that is the paper's result:

eq. 3.9  ·  WaveAbr.eic_distance
e  =  ( d + d₀ ) · ( P − P₀ )  /  ( 1 + d · d₀ )
Hopkins: "valid independent of the location of the origin and the orientation of the coordinate system employed"; and since it applies to any pair of points, the later formulae "apply to any form of surface". The Java is a direct transcription.
A correction worth making explicit. The definition is the equal-inclination condition above, which is meaningful for skew lines that never meet — and Hopkins stresses that his formulae "apply equally to skew rays". The familiar picture of two points equidistant from a crossing point is the intersecting special case, not the definition. It is still the right intuition, because it is what makes the path-difference property obvious.

Why this is the whole trick

If the two rays leave a common wavefront, the path difference accumulated as far as an equally inclined pair does not depend on which pair you choose. Writing L, L₀ for the paths as far as X, the paths to a pair at distance a from X are L + na and L₀ + na; the na cancels for every a. An EIC pair is therefore a stand-in for a common wavefront, computable from ray data alone. So n·e is an optical path difference.

Three checks that it is the right quantity

  • Intersecting rays give the equidistance result — it is not assumed. Put the crossing at X and write P = X + ad, P₀ = X + a₀d₀, with a, a₀ signed distances from X. The numerator becomes a + a(d₀·d) − a₀(d·d₀) − a₀ = (a − a₀)(1 + d·d₀), so e = a − a₀. Then P̃ sits at distance a − (a − a₀) = a₀ from X — the same distance as P₀. The isosceles picture falls out of the algebra.
  • Parallel rays (object at infinity). With d = d₀ it collapses to e = d·(P − P₀), the projection of the ray separation onto the common direction — the plane-wavefront path difference. No special case needed.
  • Nearly parallel rays, which is the normal case, are where the denominator earns its keep. Impose the condition without the sign flip — d·(P̃ − P₀) = d₀·(P̃ − P₀) — and you get a perfectly valid construction with e = (d − d₀)·(P − P₀) / (1 − d·d₀), whose denominator vanishes as the rays become parallel. Hopkins' 1 + d·d₀ = 2cos²(θ/2) tends to 2 instead. He chose the form that is best conditioned exactly where optical systems sit.

The code is the formula verbatim

double e = (r.dir.plus(r0.dir).dot(r.pt.minus(r0.pt)))
         / (1. + r.dir.dot(r0.dir));

which is ( d + d₀ )·( P − P₀ ) / ( 1 + d·d₀ ), term for term.

eq. 3.10 – 3.22
the master equation

Hopkins' assembled formula, and the Java

Equation (3.5) splits (3.1) into object space, the interior, and image space. Applying equally inclined chords to the first and last gives (3.10) and (3.11), and the result is stated twice — as (3.14), then in its computational form:

eq. 3.22
W′ = −n₁ẽ₁ − P + nkẽk + P̄ + δΩ′,     δΩ′ = −nke′
P is the aperture ray's interior sum, P̄ the principal ray's. Hopkins names Ω′ = W′ − δΩ′ the invariant aberration — the aberration referred to any sphere through E′ and B̃′ — and δΩ′ the focal-shift term.

Now the Java, from wave_abr_full_calc_finite_pup:

var opd = -n_obj*e1 - ray_op + n_img*ekp + cr_op - n_img*ep;
Term for term, this is (3.22). e1 ↔ ẽ₁ (3.12), ray_op ↔ P, ekp ↔ ẽk (3.13), cr_op ↔ P̄, -n_img*ep ↔ δΩ′. Nothing is rearranged and no sign is flipped. The value WaveAbr returns is Hopkins' W′.
RegionHopkinsJava
object → surface 1 −n₁ẽ₁  (3.10, 3.12) -n_obj*e1
surface 1 → surface k P̄ − P  (3.8, 3.18, 3.19) cr_op - ray_op
surface k → pupil +nkẽk  (3.11, 3.13) n_img*ekp
pupil → reference sphere δΩ′ = −nke′  (3.15, 4.6) -n_img*ep

Object space (eq. 3.12)

// eq 3.12
var e1 = eic_distance(new RayData(ray.get(1).p, ray.get(0).d),
                      new RayData(cr_ray.get(1).p, cr_ray.get(0).d));

The mismatched indices are deliberate. ray[1].p is the surface-1 intersection point; ray[0].d is the object-space direction. Together they are Hopkins' (X₁, Y₁, Z₁) and (L₁, M₁, N₁), and the difference is taken as aperture minus principal, matching (3.12). Because only directions and finite surface points appear, it evaluates cleanly for the test model's object distance of 1010 mm.

Image space (eq. 3.13)

// eq 3.13
var ekp = eic_distance(new RayData(Lists.get(ray,k).p, Lists.get(ray,k).d),
                       new RayData(Lists.get(cr_ray,k).p, Lists.get(cr_ray,k).d));

k = -2, the last real surface, with the directions after refraction — Hopkins' primed direction cosines (L′k, M′k, N′k).

The interior term, where the port simplifies

Hopkins does not define P as a raw path sum. From (3.8), P = Σns(Ds − ds) — the interior path minus the axial path — and (3.17)–(3.19) reduce even that to a telescoping sum of chords drawn equally inclined to the ray and the axis, evaluated by (3.20)/(3.21). He points out that the axial path Σnsds "never appears explicitly in the formulae".

The port takes the direct route. op_delta from RayTrace.trace_raw is the raw Σnidi:

if (in_gap_range(first_surf, last_surf, surf-1, false))
    opl += before.Indx * dst_b4;

With first_surf = 1 and last_surf = k this accumulates gaps 1 … k−1 — object and image space are deliberately excluded, because ẽ₁ and ẽk own them. This is the most common source of confusion when reading the routine.

The two definitions give the same answer, because the axial term cancels in the difference: P̄ − P = Σn̄(D̄−d) − Σn(D−d) = Σn̄D̄ − ΣnD. What is lost is only conditioning: the port subtracts two sums of order 10² mm to recover a difference of order 10⁻⁴ mm, which costs about six decimal digits. In double precision that leaves ten, so it does not matter — but it is why Hopkins bothered, and why eic_distance_from_axis exists.

The unused function, explained. RayTrace.eic_distance_from_axis is Hopkins (3.20)/(3.21) — equation 3.9 with the second line taken to be the axis (L₀=M₀=0, N₀=1, origin at the vertex). Nothing calls it. It is not stray code: it is the one piece of Hopkins' conditioning strategy the port chose not to implement, and it is unreachable precisely because op_delta takes the raw sum instead.

The exit pupil and the reference sphere

In the paper, E′ is where the principal ray cuts an estimated exit pupil plane at a chosen distance l̄′k = (AkE′). Hopkins is emphatic that the estimate need not be good — "It is again no matter whether the estimate of β′, or of h̄′k, is good or bad" — and (4.1) explains why: Ω′ is "the wavefront aberration referred to any sphere which passes through the points E′ and B̃′". Only the focal-shift term δΩ′ notices where you put the pupil.

The port instead solves for the pupil: it walks the chief ray to its axis crossing.

double h = b4_pt.y;
double u = b4_dir.y;
if (Math.abs(u) < 1e-14) exp_dst = exp_dst_parax;
else                     exp_dst = -h / u;

This is a legitimate specialisation rather than a contradiction — for a centred system the chief ray's axis crossing is the real exit pupil for that field point, which is a better-conditioned choice than an arbitrary estimate, and (4.1) guarantees it cannot change Ω′. For an axial field the chief ray runs along the axis and never crosses it, so u ≈ 0 and the code falls back to the paraxial fod.exp_dist.

The reference sphere then follows the paper exactly: centre at the chosen image point Q̄′, radius (E′Q̄′).

R′ = | Q̄′ − exp_pt |      ref_dir = ( Q̄′ − exp_pt ) / R′
Hopkins' (L′, M′, N′) are the direction cosines of E′Q̄′ — that is exactly ref_dir, and N′ = ref_dir.z. Remember that pairing; it matters in §5. The chief ray therefore meets the sphere at the pupil centre — verified numerically as |Q̄′−exp_pt| − R′ = 0 to machine precision.
Limitation worth knowing. transfer_to_exit_pupil uses only the y components — the commented-out lines just above show the intended x²+y² form, and the comment // handle field points in the YZ plane is accurate. Field points with a non-zero x component are not covered by this pupil-crossing solve. Hopkins' whole apparatus is built for skew principal rays, so this restriction is the port's, not his.

From the chord to the sphere

The EIC construction lands the ray at B̃′, the partner of the pupil centre E′ — not on the reference sphere. e′ = (B̃′B′) closes that gap. The code builds B̃′ first:

var dst        = ekp - cr_exp_dist;
var eic_exp_pt = b4_pt.minus(b4_dir.times(dst));
var p_coord    = eic_exp_pt.minus(cr_exp_pt);

The chief ray reaches E′ after cr_exp_dist; the ray reaches eic_exp_pt after cr_exp_dist − ekp. The travel distances differ by exactly −ekp, which is the definition of an EIC pair — the same condition Hopkins imposes in (5.6) to find B̃′'s z coordinate. p_coord is his (X̃′−X′, …), the ray's exit-pupil coordinate measured from the pupil centre; in the test system its length at full aperture is 11.3905 against a paraxial fod.exp_radius of 11.3541.

Intersecting the ray with the sphere, Hopkins' (4.2) gives the quadratic (4.3), with the auxiliary quantities (4.4) and (4.5):

eq. 4.3 – 4.5
e′² − 2(R′F)e′ + (R′J) = 0
F = ΣL̄′L′ − (1/R′)ΣL′k(X̃′−X′)      J = (1/R′)Σ(X̃′−X′)² − 2ΣL̄′(X̃′−X′)
The Java is a literal transcription of both. And J has a clean reading: J·R′ = |B̃′ − Q̄′|² − R′², i.e. how far off the sphere the EIC point already sits. It is small — of order 10−3 in the test system while F ≈ 1 — which is the whole point of the construction.

Hopkins then picks the root: "the negative sign has to be chosen, since e′=0 when B′ coincides with E′: in this case J=0". He multiplies through by the conjugate — because R′F ≈ 66 and the root ≈ 4×10−4, the direct form would subtract two numbers agreeing to eleven digits — and arrives at:

eq. 4.6
e′  =  J  /  { F + √( F² − J/R′ ) }

The Java:

var sign_soln = ref_dir.z * Lists.get(cr.ray,-1).d.z < 0 ? -1 : 1;
var denom     = F + sign_soln*Math.sqrt(F*F + J/ref_sphere_radius);
var ep        = denom == 0 ? 0.0 : J/denom;
The one substantive disagreement. Hopkins has F² − J/R′; the code has F*F + J/ref_sphere_radius. Deriving the roots of (4.3) independently confirms the paper: the discriminant must be F² − J/R′. Instrumenting the JP2018-189733 test model across three fields, the exact form lands the ray on the reference sphere to 1×10−14 mm, while the code's form misses by 2×10−9 to 2×10−7 mm. The two agree to first order in J and diverge by roughly J²/(4F³R′), so the worst-case OPD error in that model is 0.0004 waves — far below the 10−6 relative tolerance of the upstream regression tests, and invisible in a plot. The same form appears upstream in the Python, so this is inherited, not introduced by the port.

What sign_soln is standing in for

There is no outer sign in (4.6): after rationalisation the denominator is always F + √(…). Hopkins' orientation dependence lives inside the radical instead. Equation (4.21) restates (4.6) in reduced coordinates as

eq. 4.21, with (2.10), (2.11), (4.8), (4.20)
e′ = J / { F + √( F² − (N′/R′)J ) }
The paper writes the coefficient as nku′ku′kN′/H′; chaining (4.8) h′/R′ = u′k with (2.11) 1/h′ = −nku′k/H′ identifies it as −N′/R′, consistent with (4.6) whenever N′ = 1.

And N′ is the z direction cosine of E′Q̄′ — that is ref_dir.z, the very quantity sign_soln tests. So the code has taken Hopkins' N′ out of the radical and turned it into a discrete ±1 on the outside. For an ordinary system N′ ≈ +1 and the two arrangements agree; after an odd number of mirrors they do not. Writing the denominator as F + sqrt(F*F - ref_dir.z*J/R) would reproduce (4.21) directly and make sign_soln unnecessary.

Neither point is worth changing on its own — the measured error is four ten-thousandths of a wave, the regression suite pins the current values, and this is upstream behaviour. They are recorded here so the next reader who compares the code against the paper does not have to re-derive the discrepancy.

Sign convention

Because the Java reproduces (3.22) unchanged, the returned value carries (3.1)'s ordering:

opd = W′ = [ principal ray ] − [ aperture ray ]
A positive OPD means the aperture ray's optical path to the reference sphere is shorter than the chief ray's. Several later texts define W with the opposite sign; if you compare against another package, check this first.

WavefrontAberrationAnalysis.opd then scales by 1/nm_to_sys_units(wvl) to give waves, which is what the fan plots and the regression tests carry.

Biggest divergence
wave_abr_full_calc_inf_ref

Where the port stops following the paper

Everything above covers §3 and the first half of §4 — equations (3.1) through (4.6). The port implements that faithfully. It implements almost nothing of what comes after, and that is the single largest structural difference between the two.

The second half of §4 exists because (4.4) and (4.5) are not determinate as written. Hopkins says so plainly: "when the exit pupil E′ is at infinity the quantities Σ(X̃′−X′)², ΣL̄′(X̃′−X′) and R′ all become infinite." His fix is the reduced coordinates of (4.7)–(4.12), normalised by the paraxial ray height h′, leading to the determinate forms

eq. 4.25, 4.28 — not implemented
F = ΣL̄′L′ + N′u′kΣL̄′k(x̃′−x′)
and a three-term form for J built on the Lagrange invariant H′, which Hopkins notes "is always finite, and not of great magnitude". These stay bounded when h′ and R′ do not.

The Java uses the direct forms (4.4) and (4.5) with a literal ref_sphere_radius, and therefore inherits exactly the indeterminacy the reduced coordinates were invented to remove. It handles that with a branch:

if (M.is_kinda_big(ref_sphere.ref_sphere_radius))   // R > 1e8
    return wave_abr_full_calc_inf_ref(...);
else
    return wave_abr_full_calc_finite_pup(...);

and the infinite branch is not Hopkins at all. It cites Mikš, Dependence of the wave-front aberration on the radius of the reference sphere (JOSA A 19, 1187). Object space is unchanged — ẽ₁ is still (3.12) — but the sphere intersection is replaced by:

  • ray_dist_to_perp_from_origin returns d·(−p), the distance to the foot of the perpendicular from the image-space origin. Added to ray_op it gives V_B, a path referred to that plane rather than to a sphere.
  • dist_to_shortest_join finds the common perpendicular between the two rays; rF0 is its midpoint — the best available "where they cross" for skew rays.
  • W0 refers both paths to rF0; W_inf adds the shift to the actual image point, with a 1.0 + d_b4·d_cr_b4 denominator — equation 3.9 in a different costume.

So the port reaches the same destination by a different road. It is worth being clear that this is a deliberate substitution and not an omission: implementing (4.25)/(4.28) would need h′, u′k, H′ and the reduced pupil coordinates threaded through the trace, which is a much larger change than adding one branch.

08
Reading notes

Loose ends

  • Dead assignments. Both wave_abr_full_calc_* methods assign the parameter wvl from cr.wvl and then ray_pkg.wvl, and never read it. Several unpacked locals (fld, foc, cr_b4_pt, lcl_tfrm_last in the finite branch) are likewise unused — artifacts of mirroring the Python tuple unpacking.
  • The phase-element hook is commented out in trace_raw. Where upstream adds a phase contribution to op_delta for diffractive surfaces, this port has the Python in a block comment; RaySeg carries a matching // TODO phase. Hopkins' §1 lists holographic images and diffraction gratings among the cases his formulae cover, so this is a real gap rather than a hypothetical one.
  • Ordering constraint. opd() reads fld.chief_ray and fld.ref_sphere off the field object. Those are populated by setup_pupil_coords, so the chief ray must be traced and the sphere built before any ray's OPD can be evaluated.
